How this works

v6 was a cheat sheet — built for a 90-second scan before Mehdi's call. v7 is different. It's a deliberate-practice tool. It only works if you speak out loud, repeatedly, over the next two weeks.

The gap to close

Mehdi flagged four traits that mark a successful hire: good memory, good presentation skills, solid PM know-how, ability to pivot. The one that's furthest from how you came across in the screening is solid PM know-how — not because the experience isn't there, but because you describe your work in outcomes language ("we launched", "I delivered") rather than PM craft language ("I held the risk register", "stage-gate review at design freeze", "controlled scope variation absorbed without schedule slip").

Same projects. Same facts. Different register. The dep manager will recognise the second register and not the first.

The plan

  • Translator (the core): 30+ paired cards. Your natural phrasing on top, hidden PM register below. Don't read both — produce the PM version aloud first, then reveal to check. Mark each one ✓ sticky, ~ shaky, or ✗ stuck. Drill the shakies until they're sticky.
  • STAR stories: Moza Banco and Mulala, fully rebuilt in PM register. Recall mode hides Action and Result so you have to retell from memory.
  • Question drill: 12 anticipated questions weighted by JD priority (40% PM oversight / 25% planning / 25% change management / 10% budget). Read the question, answer aloud, expand the scaffold to check what you missed.
  • Voice cues: Pronunciation slips and rhythm reminders carried over from v6.

Daily practice routine (15–20 min)

  1. Open the Translator tab. Filter to Shaky + Stuck (skip what's already sticky).
  2. Drill 8–10 cards aloud. Reveal, check, mark.
  3. Open one STAR story. Retell from the Situation/Task only. Reveal Action/Result to check.
  4. Pick 2 questions from the question drill. Answer aloud. Expand to check.
  5. End session. The tracker logs your reps.
Why this matters more than you think. The dep manager will probably not ask you a single question you haven't already answered for some other interviewer at some point. The difference between passing and not passing isn't what you say — it's the register you say it in. PM register surfaces under pressure only if you've drilled it under no pressure first.
Risk to manage. Don't over-rehearse. Senior PMs sound a little casual when they describe their work — it's familiar to them. If your answers come out polished and rhythmic, they read as prepared, which paradoxically reads as less senior. The goal is to make PM register natural, not performed.

Two weeks out — week-by-week

Week 1: Heavy Translator work. Mark and drill. End the week with most cards at "sticky." Light STAR work — just read each story once aloud per day.

Week 2: STAR retelling becomes the focus. Question drill twice. Translator becomes maintenance only — the shakies that didn't stick.

Day before: Read the round-2 brief. Skim sticky translations. Don't drill anything new. Sleep.

Moza Banco · Maputo · Under 3 months kickoff to demo · Sole product lead · Board-sponsored greenfield

PreBank was one of several products in flight at Moza Banco during my 2015–2016 tenure. "Under $50K for three native platforms in three months — what was the scope really?" Demo-stage, deliberately scoped. "How does this map to a MedTech environment?" The shape of the regulatory dependency transfers, though the specific function is different. Deferral isn't failure when the deferral is itself the decision the demo enabled.

Why this matters first

Every probe a Nipro dep manager will throw at you on "how would you actually run a medical device project" routes through design controls. It's the operational backbone — the difference between "I understand regulations exist" and "I understand how a PM operationalises them inside a project plan." Your ISO 13485 cert touches this; this module builds the layer underneath that cert.

The seven elements (memorise this)

21 CFR 820.30 (FDA) and ISO 13485 §7.3 both describe the same process under slightly different names. Seven elements, in sequence:

  1. Design and development planning — the project plan itself. Identifies interfaces between groups, assigns responsibilities, gets reviewed and updated as design evolves. This is where PM lives.
  2. Design inputs — user needs, intended use, regulatory requirements, applicable standards. Locked, signed off, traceable. The single most common failure point in MedTech projects: weak inputs.
  3. Design outputs — drawings, specifications, software code, labelling. Each output traces back to an input.
  4. Design review — formal stage-gate meetings at planned milestones. Cross-functional. Documented. Independent reviewer required.
  5. Design verification — "did we build the thing right?" Testing that outputs meet inputs. Bench testing, code review, design analysis.
  6. Design validation — "did we build the right thing?" Testing under actual or simulated use conditions, with the actual users where possible. Includes clinical evaluation for higher-risk devices.
  7. Design transfer — moving the validated design into production. Manufacturing instructions, training, process validation.

And underneath all seven: design changes (controlled change procedure) and the Design History File (DHF) — the evidentiary record of all of the above.

The V-model. Search "V-model medical device design controls" — find one good diagram, screenshot it. Left arm descending: User Needs → Design Inputs → System Specs → Detail Design → Implementation. Right arm ascending: Unit Test → Integration Test → Verification → Validation. The two arms connect at each level — every design input has a corresponding verification activity. Internalise this shape. It is the single most useful visual for talking PM-of-a-medical-device-project credibly.

Verification vs. validation — get this right

This is the question that catches non-MedTech PMs. The trap is to treat them as synonyms.

  • Verification = "design output meets design input." Engineering/objective. Did the device do what the spec said it should do?
  • Validation = "design meets user needs." User/clinical. Does the device do what the user actually needs in real-world conditions?

You can pass verification and fail validation. (Spec was wrong.) You can fail verification and not even reach validation. (Build was wrong.) Both are mandatory. Both produce documented evidence. Both feed the DHF.

The Design History File (DHF)

Not a folder. A controlled, auditable record of the entire design controls process. Inputs, outputs, reviews, V&V results, change records, risk records, traceability matrix. Required by 21 CFR 820.30(j). The DHF is the artefact a PM owns — not produces at the end, but maintains throughout. If you're doing it right, the DHF is current at every stage gate; if you're doing it wrong, you're scrambling to backfill it before submission.

The three D-files. Every MedTech PM must know all three by name: DHF (Design History File) — the controlled record of the design process; DMR (Device Master Record) — the definitive "recipe" for manufacturing (drawings, specs, procedures, acceptance criteria); DHR (Device History Record) — the auditable production diary for each batch or unit (dates, quantities, acceptance records, UDI). The DHF proves you designed it right; the DMR tells anyone how to build it; the DHR proves each unit was built to the DMR. Nipro ships physical devices — the DMR and DHR are where manufacturing and quality live.
The PM-craft framing. Stage gates in your project plan map directly to design reviews. The traceability matrix (user need → input → output → V&V evidence → risk control) is your living single-source-of-truth. Risk register and design changes feed each other — every change triggers a risk reassessment. Verification and validation aren't testing phases tacked on at the end; they're planned into the schedule from day one with their own resource and lead-time profile.
After this module, you'll be able to say
"I'd structure the project around the design controls process — inputs locked at the input review, outputs traced to inputs, V&V planned into the schedule from day one, DHF maintained as we go rather than backfilled at the end. Stage gates would map to design reviews — input review, output review, V&V review, transfer review — with cross-functional sign-off and an independent reviewer at each. The traceability matrix is the artefact I'd own most actively, linking user needs through inputs and outputs to V&V evidence and risk controls."

Self-test — before marking this module done

1. What is the difference between design verification and design validation?
Verification = output meets input (engineering, objective — "did we build the thing right?"). Validation = design meets user needs in real or simulated conditions (clinical/user — "did we build the right thing?"). You can pass one and fail the other; both are mandatory; both feed the DHF.
2. Name the seven elements of design controls in order.
Planning → Inputs → Outputs → Review → Verification → Validation → Transfer. Plus design changes (controlled change procedure) and the DHF underpinning everything.
3. What is the DHF, and what does a PM actually do with it during a project?
Design History File. The controlled, auditable record of the design controls process: inputs, outputs, reviews, V&V results, change records, traceability matrix. Required by 21 CFR 820.30(j). The PM maintains it throughout the project (current at every stage gate), not at the end. If you're scrambling to assemble it before submission, you've already failed the audit.
4. A stakeholder asks you "why are we doing a design review now, can't we just keep building?" What's the PM answer?
Design reviews are a regulatory requirement (21 CFR 820.30(e) / ISO 13485 §7.3.5), but the operational reason is risk reduction — they catch input-output gaps before they become rework, with cross-functional sign-off and an independent reviewer. Skipping or compressing them transfers risk forward in the project where rework is more expensive and may invalidate prior verification work. The review is the cheapest way to fail-fast.
5. Name the three D-files and what each one proves.
DHF (Design History File) — proves the device was designed correctly through controlled records of inputs, outputs, reviews, V&V, and traceability. DMR (Device Master Record) — the manufacturing "recipe" containing drawings, specs, procedures, and acceptance criteria so any qualified facility can reproduce the device. DHR (Device History Record) — the auditable production diary for each batch/unit proving it was built to the DMR (dates, quantities, acceptance records, UDI).

The shape of the standard

ISO 14971:2019 is the standard for "Application of risk management to medical devices." Don't memorise the standard — memorise the process flow it mandates and the artefacts it produces.

The risk management process

  1. Risk management planning — scope, criteria, responsibilities, methods. Documented up front, signed off.
  2. Risk analysis — identify hazards (sources of harm), identify foreseeable sequences of events, estimate probability of harm and severity of harm.
  3. Risk evaluation — compare estimated risks against the criteria you set in planning. Acceptable? Acceptable with controls? Unacceptable?
  4. Risk control — apply control measures, in priority order: (1) inherently safe design, (2) protective measures in the device or in manufacturing, (3) information for safety (labelling, training). This priority is not optional. You cannot label your way out of a design defect.
  5. Residual risk evaluation — after controls are applied, what risk remains? Is it acceptable? Risk-benefit analysis when residual risk is non-trivial.
  6. Risk management review — formal review, documented, signed off. Produces the Risk Management Report.
  7. Production and post-production information — feedback from the field updates the risk file. Risk management never stops while the device is on the market.
Hazard ≠ harm ≠ risk. Hazard = potential source of harm (e.g. exposed sharp edge). Harm = physical injury or damage (e.g. laceration). Risk = combination of probability of occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm. Get this language right and you sound credible immediately.
FMEA is not ISO 14971. This is a dangerous and frequent industry misconception. FMEA focuses on component failure modes — what happens when a part breaks. ISO 14971 requires analysis of all hazards, including those from normal, fault-free use (biocompatibility reactions, use errors, human-machine interface risks). FMEA alone does not satisfy the standard. A complete risk file needs both: FMEA for component-level failure analysis, plus ISO 14971 for the full hazard landscape including foreseeable misuse and normal-use risks.

The Risk Management File (RMF)

Like the DHF, the RMF is a living artefact maintained throughout the lifecycle. Contains: risk management plan, hazard analysis, risk estimation, risk control records, residual risk evaluation, risk management report, post-production data. Cross-references to the DHF heavily — every risk control measure should map to a design output and a verification activity.

The PM-craft framing. Risk management runs in parallel to design controls, not sequentially. Hazards are identified at the input stage, risk controls are designed into the outputs, controls are verified during V&V, residual risk is evaluated at transfer. The RMF is owned cross-functionally — typically led by a quality or risk function, but the PM coordinates the cadence with design reviews and ensures every risk control has a verification owner and a deadline.

"I'd treat the risk management file as a project artefact owned by the cross-functional team, running in parallel to design controls. Hazards identified at design input stage, controls designed into outputs, controls verified during V&V, residual risk evaluated at transfer. The control hierarchy isn't optional — inherently safe design first, protective measures second, labelling and training only as a last resort. Self-test

1. Define hazard, harm, and risk in ISO 14971 terms.
Hazard = potential source of harm. Harm = physical injury or damage. Risk = combination of probability of occurrence of harm and severity of that harm. (The exposed sharp edge is the hazard, the laceration is the harm, the risk is the combination of likelihood × severity.)
2. What is the mandatory priority order for risk control measures?
(1) Inherently safe design, (2) protective measures in the device or manufacturing process, (3) information for safety (labelling, training, instructions for use). You cannot skip the higher level for convenience — the standard requires you to demonstrate that higher-priority measures were considered and found impracticable before relying on the next level.
3. How does risk management interact with design controls in your project plan?
Parallel, not sequential. Hazards at input stage, controls designed into outputs, controls verified during V&V, residual risk evaluated at transfer. RMF and DHF cross-reference heavily. Every risk control should map to a design output and a verification activity. Every design change triggers a risk reassessment.
4. Why is FMEA alone insufficient for ISO 14971 compliance?
FMEA analyses component failure modes — what breaks. ISO 14971 requires analysis of all hazards, including those from normal, fault-free use: biocompatibility reactions, use errors, human-machine interface risks. FMEA misses hazards that don't involve component failure. You need both: FMEA for component-level analysis plus ISO 14971 for the full hazard landscape.

What IEC 62304 actually is

"Medical device software — software lifecycle processes." The standard that defines how you develop and maintain software that's part of a medical device, or that is a medical device (Software as a Medical Device, SaMD). It's the software-specific layer that sits inside design controls — design controls tell you the project process; IEC 62304 tells you the software process.

Software safety classification (memorise this)

IEC 62304 classifies software into three classes based on the harm a failure could cause:

  • Class A — no injury or damage to health is possible. Lowest rigour. Many lifecycle activities are not required.
  • Class B — non-serious injury is possible. Medium rigour.
  • Class C — death or serious injury is possible. Highest rigour. Full lifecycle requirements apply.

The classification drives everything else. The architecture documentation required, the unit testing required, the integration testing required, the change control rigour, the maintenance plan rigour. Get the classification right early — getting it wrong late is expensive.

The most useful sentence on classification. "Software classification is a risk-based decision, made jointly with the risk management process. You classify based on the worst foreseeable harm a software failure could cause assuming all external risk controls are absent — meaning you can't classify down by saying 'a hardware lockout will catch it.' You classify the software on its own."

The lifecycle processes

  1. Software development planning — the SDP. Covers methodology, deliverables, integration with system-level design controls.
  2. Software requirements analysis — translates system requirements into software requirements.
  3. Software architectural design — high-level structure.
  4. Software detailed design — required for Class C; recommended for Class B.
  5. Software unit implementation and verification — code + unit tests.
  6. Software integration and integration testing — required for Class B and C.
  7. Software system testing — verify the software meets its requirements.
  8. Software release — controlled release with documented configuration.

SOUP — Software Of Unknown Provenance

Probably the most distinctive concept in IEC 62304 and the one that most clearly tells you whether someone has actually worked with the standard. SOUP = software you didn't develop yourself and for which you don't have full lifecycle records — typically third-party libraries, OS components, frameworks. The standard requires you to:

  • Identify all SOUP in your software
  • Specify the functional and performance requirements you need from the SOUP
  • Specify the system hardware and software requirements the SOUP needs
  • Track and evaluate published SOUP anomalies (vulnerabilities, bugs) for safety impact

Every npm dependency, every open-source library, every database driver — SOUP. This is why MedTech software teams maintain a SOUP register and have explicit policies on dependency updates.

AAMI TIR45 — Agile in regulated MedTech. The standard question you'll get: "How do you reconcile Agile with stage-gate?" The answer is AAMI TIR45, which maps Agile practices to IEC 62304 through layered iteration loops: Product (months/years) → Release (months) → Increment (weeks) → Story (days). Each layer maps to specific IEC 62304 clauses. In this model, "Definition of Done" expands to include documentation and QMS compliance — a task isn't done when code compiles, but when the evidence record is complete. That's how Agile software teams stay regulatorily compliant.
The PM-craft framing. "On a real IEC 62304 project I'd want to ramp on three things first": the project's software safety classification (drives everything downstream), the SOUP register and the policy for evaluating dependency anomalies, and the integration between the software development plan and the system-level design controls. The design controls process is what I'd lead from; IEC 62304 is the software-specific rigour layer that the engineering lead would own technically and I'd coordinate at programme level."
After this module — the upgraded gap-acknowledgment

What I do understand is the structure — software safety classification (A/B/C) drives the rigour of every lifecycle process, SOUP management is non-trivial and continuous, and the software development plan integrates inside system-level design controls rather than running parallel to them. On a real project I'd partner closely with the technical lead on classification decisions and SOUP policy, while I run the programme-level coordination, change control, and stage-gate integration with the broader design controls process."

What this module is for

You already have the Alison EU MDR cert. That demonstrates you understand the regulation exists. The PM-relevant MDR concepts

  • Technical Documentation (Annex II and III). The full evidentiary package required for CE marking. Heavily overlaps with the DHF — most of the DHF feeds the technical documentation.
  • Clinical Evaluation (Article 61, Annex XIV). Not the same as clinical trials. A documented procedure to evaluate clinical data — yours and from equivalent devices. PMs coordinate; clinical/regulatory functions own.
  • Post-Market Surveillance (Article 83 onwards). Active, systematic gathering of data from devices on the market. Feeds back into risk management. PMs maintain the cadence and the project-side artefacts.
  • UDI (Article 27). Unique Device Identification. Project deliverable: every device variant has a UDI assigned, registered, on the labelling, in EUDAMED.
  • Economic Operators (Articles 11–14). Manufacturer, Authorised Representative, Importer, Distributor — each has obligations. PMs need to know which operator owns what.
  • Notified Body. Third-party organisation that conducts conformity assessment for higher-risk devices. PMs coordinate the schedule with the notified body — submission dates, audit dates, response windows. NB delays are a major real-world risk.
The single most useful linkage. Design controls + risk management + IEC 62304 produce most of the technical documentation MDR requires. The PM's job is to ensure the project artefacts are produced in the form the technical documentation needs them — same format, same level of detail, same traceability. If you do that as you go, MDR submission becomes assembly. If you don't, it becomes a 6-month rework project.
The Stratification Gap — a CER failure mode. A notorious EU MDR rejection case: a surgical device manufacturer claimed broad clinical utility by generically cross-referencing literature across several disparate specialties, without compiling focused empirical data on their specific device. The "Stratification Gap" — claiming broad uses while neglecting device-specific evidence — caused formal rejection. The lesson: CER data must be strictly bounded to the device's exact intended use and patient population. Generic literature cross-referencing across specialties is not evidence.
The PM-craft framing. "MDR doesn't change what I do as a PM, it changes the artefact rigour and the evidentiary chain. I'd structure the project so design controls outputs feed Annex II technical documentation directly, risk management outputs feed Annex I GSPR conformity, and post-market surveillance plans are written into the project plan from the start, not bolted on at submission. The notified body interaction is its own workstream — submission windows, audit prep, response cadence — and I'd build that into the schedule with 20–30% explicit float because NB response times are not under our control. Current reality: 13–18 months for a standard device, up to 24 months for innovative Class III technology. The path isn't linear — inspectors generate hundreds of corrective questions, and the dossier stalls while the builder reiterates, rethinks, or repeats studies."
After this module, you'll be able to say

The DHF, RMF, software lifecycle records, and clinical evaluation plan all feed Annex II directly. Notified body coordination is a workstream of its own with explicit float on the schedule because response times aren't ours to control. The integrated mental model

This is the picture you want in your head when someone says "tell me how you'd run a medical device development project":

  1. Design Controls is the project process. Inputs → Outputs → Review → V&V → Transfer. DHF maintained throughout.
  2. Risk Management (ISO 14971) runs in parallel. Hazards at input stage, controls in outputs, controls verified during V&V. RMF maintained throughout.
  3. IEC 62304 sits inside design controls for the software portion. Classification drives rigour. SOUP managed continuously.
  4. EU MDR (or FDA equivalent) is the regulatory destination. Technical Documentation assembled from DHF + RMF + software records + clinical evaluation. Notified Body coordination is its own workstream.
  5. Post-market surveillance feeds back into risk management for the device's full market life. The project doesn't end at launch.

12 concepts to lock in before Round 3

  1. Design Controls sequence: Plan → Input → Output → Review → Verify → Validate → Transfer → Change → DHF.
  2. Three D-files: DHF (history), DMR (master recipe), DHR (batch record). Know all three by name.
  3. Risk Control Priority: Inherent safe design → Protective measures → Information for safety (labelling last).
  4. 510(k) Equivalence: Same intended use + same tech characteristics, OR different but no new questions + as safe and effective.
  5. EU MDR Classification: I, IIa, IIb, III based on Annex VIII rules. No grandfathering for legacy devices.
  6. Software Safety Classes: A (no injury), B (non-serious), C (serious/death). Classification drives everything downstream.
  7. V&V distinction: Verification = build it right (outputs vs inputs). Validation = build the right thing (device vs user needs).
  8. TPLC Philosophy: Safety is a continuum from concept through obsolescence, not just at market authorization.
  9. CER Failure Mode — Stratification Gap: Claiming broad uses without device-specific empirical data kills submissions.
  10. EU MDR Buffer Rule: Add 20–30% timeline buffer for Notified Body bottlenecks. Current reality: 13–24 months.
  11. AAMI TIR45: Layered iteration loops mapping Agile to IEC 62304. Definition of Done includes QMS compliance.
  12. FMEA ≠ ISO 14971: FMEA covers component failures; ISO 14971 requires analysis of normal-use hazards too.

Anti-patterns to avoid (these mark you as non-MedTech)

  • Treating verification and validation as synonyms. They aren't.
  • Saying "we tested it" instead of specifying V&V scope.
  • Saying "we pivoted hard" — pivoting in MedTech is controlled change with risk reassessment, not vibe.
  • Calling design reviews "checkpoint meetings."
  • Promising to "move fast and break things" — the regulatory environment doesn't reward that, and signalling it disqualifies you.
  • Assuming risk management is something the quality team does separately. It runs in parallel and is owned cross-functionally.
  • Talking about labelling as a fix for a design issue. Control hierarchy puts labelling last for a reason.

Round-3 expert questions — drill these aloud

Three questions from the expert-level dataset that map directly to your experience. Answer each aloud before checking the scaffold.

Q1. How do you balance Agile software development with hardware stage-gate milestones?
Hardware runs on stage-gate (long lead times, tooling, physical testing). Software runs on Agile (iterative, fast cycles). The integration point is AAMI TIR45: layered iteration loops map Agile sprints to IEC 62304 lifecycle processes. Hardware milestones (design freeze, verification, transfer) become the gates; software sprints feed into them. The PM coordinates the interface: software deliverables must be ready for hardware integration at each gate, and hardware changes must trigger software impact assessments. In a hybrid team, senior safety and regulatory members are embedded in sprints to prevent prototypes from failing regulatory review later.
Q2. How would you handle a critical supplier failure three months before launch?
Immediate containment: quarantine affected inventory, assess scope of nonconformance. Activate the secondary supplier if qualified; if not, escalate to the quality agreement's change-notification clause. Document everything in the CAPA system. Assess impact on the DMR — if the supplier's component is in the DMR, any substitution requires design change control, potential re-verification, and possibly regulatory notification. The PM runs the impact assessment workstream: schedule slip, budget impact, regulatory risk, and a recovery plan with explicit decision points. If the supplier was sole-source, that's a procurement risk that should have been on the risk register from day one — the lesson is about supplier qualification depth, not just the immediate fix.
Q3. How do you ensure design changes maintain traceability and regulatory compliance?
Every design change triggers three parallel assessments: impact on safety and effectiveness (risk management file update), impact on regulatory submission (does it require a new 510(k) or NB notification?), and impact on the traceability matrix (which inputs, outputs, V&V evidence, and risk controls are affected?). The change is classified by level: Level 1 (minor, internal approval), Level 2 (moderate, requires verification), Level 3 (major, requires verification, validation, and potentially regulatory submission). The PM ensures the traceability matrix is updated in real time — not backfilled — so the DHF remains current. In a Live Traceability system, the impact assessment is instant; in spreadsheets, it's manual and error-prone. The rule: no change is implemented until the impact assessment is complete and approved.

If asked: "what would you do in your first 90 days"

A defensible answer
"First 30 days — listen mode. Read the project plan, the DHF, the RMF, the change log. Meet every cross-functional lead — engineering, regulatory, quality, clinical, manufacturing, the notified body interface. Understand the active risks and the open design changes. Don't propose anything yet. Days 30–60 — synthesis. Where are inputs ambiguous? Where is V&V evidence thin? Where is the traceability matrix breaking down? What's the next stage gate and what's the gap to readiness? Days 60–90 — first interventions, surgical and consultative. One stage gate run cleanly. One change control improvement. One reporting rhythm tightened. Don't try to remake the process. Build credibility through one or two visible wins, then earn the right to propose larger changes."

If asked: "what's the biggest difference between MedTech PM and digital/agency PM"

A defensible answer
"Evidentiary discipline. In agency or pure-digital work, the artefact is the product — the page, the campaign, the launch. In MedTech the artefact is the evidentiary chain behind the product, because that chain is what makes the product legal to ship and safe to use. The PM's daily work is the same — scope, schedule, risk, stakeholders, change — but the standard for what counts as 'done' is much higher, and most of the work is producing artefacts that prove what you did, not just doing it."

  • Good memory — fluency with project history without notes. Names, decisions, dates, stakeholders. Auditability lives in your head before the document. Regulated-environment tell.
  • Good presentation skills — executive readouts, steering committees, VP updates. Translating technical complexity upward. The role explicitly mentions presenting to executives.
  • Solid PM know-how — methodology fluency. Risk register, RAID log, change control, stage gates, governance artefacts. This is the gap to close.
  • Ability to pivot — the most diagnostic. Tells you the environment is not clean waterfall execution. Things move. Blockers appear. Regulatory feedback loops back. The PM reroutes without losing the plot.

Day-of dep manager round — final checklist

  • Sleep ≥ 7 hours. Skim sticky translations. No new drills.
  • Speak the opener aloud once, slowly. Four landing beats.
  • Pre-warm the voice for 5 minutes before the call (read this section aloud).
  • Water within reach. Pen and notebook.
  • Resist the urge to over-prepare. The work was the previous two weeks.

Questions to ask them — the closing 5 minutes

"Do you have any questions for me?" lands at the end of every round. A weak answer here can undo 40 minutes of strong work. Prepare three. Pick the ones that fit the conversation — don't ask all three mechanically.

"What does success at the 90-day mark look like for the person in this role?" Signals you're thinking about delivery, not just landing the offer. Their answer also tells you what they're actually measuring — which is useful intel for later rounds.
"How does the PM working group share knowledge across product lines — dialysis, infusion, pharma packaging? Is there a cross-product PM rhythm, or does each line run independently?" Shows you've thought about Nipro's product mix and you're already thinking like a Digital Ecosystem PM (cross-product, not single-product). Their answer also tells you whether this is a collaborative environment or a siloed one — useful for you, not just performative.
"Where do you see the digital ecosystem investment heading over the next 24 months? Is this role primarily executing on a defined roadmap, or shaping one that's still forming?" Tests how strategic the role actually is vs how it reads in the JD. The answer changes how you'd approach the work. Also signals you're evaluating the role as a long-term fit, not a stepping stone.
"From your perspective, what's the hardest part of running a programme like this at Nipro — what tends to break first under pressure?" Backup question if the conversation has already covered roadmap and team. Invites a real answer, not a corporate one. Often the most revealing question you can ask a hiring manager.
What not to ask. Don't ask about salary, holidays, hybrid policy, or anything HR-shaped — that's Mehdi territory, not the dep manager's. Don't ask "what does Nipro do?" — basic homework. Don't ask anything you could have Googled. And don't ask more than two or three questions; closing the call cleanly matters more than fitting in a fourth.
One last thing. The dep manager interview is a craft conversation, not a stress test. They're trying to see if they'd want you in their team. The instinct to perform under that lens is exactly what makes candidates over-rehearse and sound robotic. Be the person you'd want to work with — calm, specific, honest about what you've done and what you haven't, willing to think out loud when a question doesn't fit a rehearsed answer. That's what "solid PM know-how" actually looks like to the people who recognise it.
